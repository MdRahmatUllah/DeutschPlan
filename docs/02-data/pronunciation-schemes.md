# Pronunciation schemes: German spelt for English, Russian and Polish readers

A meaning language's `Pronunciation (X)` column in *All Words* (#1080's convention) spells each German word in the reader's own alphabet, so a learner with no IPA can say it aloud. This page fixes how: one spelling for each German sound, and one way to mark stress. The Bangla guide (`Pronunciation (Bangla)`, key on each workbook's Guide tab) is the model: *ü → ্যু, ö → ্যো, ch after i/e → ইশ, after a/o/u → খ, final r → আ, final b/d/g → p/t/k*. It is unchanged.

Rules for every scheme:

- The guide spells the **German cell only**, never the article: *das Abendessen* → `AH-bent-es-sen`.
- **Standard German**, as a careful speaker says it: final b/d/g are hardened, -ig ends in the ich sound, r after a vowel at the end of a word or word part is vocalised.
- **One sound, one spelling.** A reviewer can check a row against the tables below; a sound spelt two ways is a finding.
- A doubled consonant in German is written once (Russian, Polish) or split across the syllable break (English `BIT-tuh`, which only shows the short vowel: say the t once).

The pilot (A1 weeks 1–2, 98 words) follows these tables. The reviewers (#1082 agent-3, #1083 agent-2, #1084 agent-1) approve each scheme before the bulk work.

## English (#1082)

A respelling in ordinary English letters. **Stress:** syllables are joined by hyphens and the stressed syllable is in capitals. A one-word entry always has one capitalised syllable, even when it has only one (`IHY`, `YAH`). In a phrase, each word that carries stress has one; small unstressed words (articles, pronouns, prepositions, auxiliaries) stay lowercase: `owf VEE-der-zay-en`, `ess toot meer LAIT`.

Every `r` in the respelling follows one rule: **starting a syllable it is the German throat r; ending a syllable it is silent, as in British *beer*, *more*, *fur*.** That is what lets `ur` (ö), `er` (final -er) and `BEER` (Bier) work.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| i (short) | i | *bit* | bitte → `BIT-tuh` |
| i, ie, ih (long) | ee | *see* | wie → `VEE` |

## Russian (#1083)

Cyrillic, as a Russian reader says it. **Stress:** an acute accent (U+0301, combining) on the stressed vowel of every word with more than one vowel letter: `би́тэ`, `а́уф ви́дазээн`. A one-vowel word carries none (`ихь`, `шён`), nor does a word whose stressed vowel is ё.

German e is written **э**, never е, so the consonant before it stays hard (`гэт`, not `гет`): softening consonants before e and i is the typical Russian accent in German. е, я, ё, ю appear only for German j + vowel (`е́да` = *jeder*) and after the soft хь.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| a | а | | Abend → `а́бэнт` |

| i, ie, ih | и | | wie → `ви` |
| o, oo, oh | о | a clear о, also unstressed (never а) | wo → `во` |
| u, uh | у | | Buch → `бух` |
| ü, üh, y | ю | say и with lips rounded for у | Tschüss → `чюс` |
| ö, öh | ё | say э with lips rounded for о | schön → `шён` |

| au | ау | one syllable: а glides into у | auf → `а́уф` |
| eu, äu | ой | | heute → `хо́йтэ` |
| j + vowel | е, я, ё, ю; й elsewhere | | ja → `я`, jeder → `е́да` |
| -er ending a word or word part | а | | jeder → `е́да`, wieder- → `ви́да-` |
| r after a vowel, ending a word or word part | а | | Bier → `би́а`, mir → `ми́а` |

| ch after a, o, u, au | х | | Buch → `бух` |

| chs | кс | | sechs → `зэкс` |

## Polish (#1084)

Polish spelling for German sounds. **Stress:** syllables are joined by hyphens and the stressed syllable is in capitals, as in the English guide: `auf WI-da-ze-en`, `ICH`, `es tut mija LAJT`. #1084 proposed underlining the stressed syllable in the cell; a cell's underline is formatting, which the pipeline does not carry into `content.db` and the app cannot show, so capitals take its place (the reviewer and the owner decide).

Polish reads *zi, si, ci, ni* as ź, ś, ć, ń, and *i* before a vowel as a softening sign. So in this guide **zi, si, ci, ni, di, ti are hard**, as in foreign words (*taxi*, *Zimbabwe*), and *i* before a vowel is written **ij** (`BIJA`, `mija`).

## Open questions for the reviewers

- **English ich sound:** `hy` (*huge*) keeps it apart from `sh`, as #1082 asks; the course's own grammar rule and the Bangla guide say *ish*. `sh` is easier to read, `hy` is the right sound.
- **Polish ich sound:** `ch` with the note, as #1084 proposes; `ś` (`IŚ`, `MET-śen`) would need no note but is a different sound.
- **Polish stress:** capitals instead of underlining (above).
- **Russian e:** `э` everywhere (accurate, keeps consonants hard) rather than the more familiar `е`.

## How the columns get into the workbooks

Drafts are staging JSON in the git-ignored `data/_staging/<code>/<workbook stem>.json`: each word keyed by its German, POS and English cells (one row per key in every workbook), each grammar topic by Level and Topic. `tools/merge_language_columns.py` writes them into a copy of the workbook:

```
python tools/merge_language_columns.py data/German_A1_Tracker.xlsx en ru pl \
    --out data/_staging/pilot/German_A1_Tracker.xlsx
```

New columns go after the existing ones, styled like their English column; a second merge rewrites them in place. It never writes its input, puts the Dashboard chart back (openpyxl rewrites it on save), and then checks the output: every cell outside the new columns and the chart parts must equal the input's, or the output is deleted. `tools/tests/test_merge_language_columns.py` covers it.
